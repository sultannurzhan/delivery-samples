# The Smallest Waiting Room

Original AI-assisted fiction prepared solely as a production demonstration. No client manuscript or personal biography was used.

## 1. The Key

The key to the old station arrived in a biscuit tin. Mara found it beneath three buttons and a note from the village clerk: Please see what can be kept.

The trains had stopped years before. Rain now entered through a cracked pane, and the timetable listed places that sounded farther away than they were. Mara opened the waiting room and stood still until the dust settled.

There were two wooden benches, a round clock, and a cupboard that smelled of paper. Inside the cupboard lay a ledger. Its columns recorded tickets, parcels, and, on one unexpected page, things passengers had left behind.

A blue glove. A bag of pears. One small telescope.

Mara turned the page. Someone had drawn a careful star beside the telescope and written, Collected before the evening train.

She had expected a room full of rubbish. Instead, she found evidence of people who had trusted this little building to keep something safe. She carried the benches outside, borrowed a broom, and began with the floor.

## 2. The Shelf

On Tuesday, the carpenter brought a spare pane of glass. On Wednesday, a schoolteacher arrived with six books and no particular plan. Mara put them on the windowsill while she cleaned the cupboard.

By Friday, someone had taken a book and left two others. A pencilled note appeared beneath them: May these stay?

Mara asked the clerk, who consulted a folder, frowned at an empty form, and finally said that books weighed less than abandoned furniture. The windowsill became a shelf. The shelf became three.

They kept both benches facing the window. No one wanted the room to resemble a shop. There would be no counter and no opening bell, only a small sign asking visitors to close the door when rain came from the east.

The clock could not be repaired cheaply. Mara left its hands at twenty past four, the time written beside the last train in the ledger. Then she placed an ordinary kitchen clock below it, so anyone waiting for a friend would still know when that friend was late.

## 3. A Place to Wait

The first cold afternoon brought five visitors. One read a gardening manual. Two played a quiet game of cards. A child traced the old railway line on a map while his grandfather named the villages along it.

Mara sat by the door, unsure whether she ought to be organising something. A reading circle, perhaps, or a speech about the station. She had written three sentences on a card that morning. Now the card felt unnecessary.

Outside, a cyclist stopped to mend a loose strap. Through the window he saw the benches and asked whether the room was open to everyone.

“Yes,” Mara said. “You can just come in.”

He stayed until the rain passed. Before leaving, he tightened the loose screw in the door handle with a tool from his bag.

That evening, Mara added one line to the old ledger: A dry place, returned to use.

She put the key in the biscuit tin. The clerk would open tomorrow. For the first time all week, Mara went home without carrying a list.
